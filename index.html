Injection mold tooling

Design Engineering Intern  |  Saudi Mais for Medical Products, Riyadh  |  June to August 2026

Mold designs went from drawing straight to machining. Nobody held the geometry before steel was cut, so mistakes surfaced after the tooling was already made. Several parts had no drawings or model on file at all.

Step one

What I was handed

The physical bicarbonate bag holder and cap, and nothing else. No drawings, no model, no dimensions on file. Before anyone could change the part or build tooling for it, the geometry had to exist somewhere.

Step two

Measured the whole assembly by hand

Calipers and micrometers, feature by feature, including the inner filters. Tolerances ran from 0.1 mm on the caps and bodies down to 0.02 mm where parts meet the needle, so the measuring had to be tighter than the loosest feature would suggest.

Step three

Rebuilt it in CAD, with drawings behind it

Every measured feature modelled and dimensioned. Left, an isometric of the whole assembly. Right, the top view of the cap, bail and spout. Parts made from this model fit and worked, which is the only real test of whether the measuring was right.

Step four

Printed the mold geometry before cutting steel

Instead of releasing a drawing straight to the machine shop, I printed the mold geometry in PLA and checked it in my hands first. It went onto six molds carrying roughly $900,000 of tooling, and the company still runs the step.

Step five

Checked everyone else's drawings before release

Flatness, hole concentricity and wall thickness on each release package. I also added ISO 286 fits and GD&T callouts to drawings that had dimensions but nothing controlling how the parts actually had to go together.

Machining a national-winning car

Manufacturing Engineer  |  Vestyge Racing, STEM Racing, Riyadh  |  November 2024 to July 2025

A car cut from a polyurethane block has to hit a surface finish target, a mass window and dimensional tolerances at once. Roughness costs you drag, mass costs you acceleration, and material removed to help one hurts the other.

Step one

Wrote the toolpaths

Three machining orientations, each with its own plan, generated in QuickCAM Pro. A 6.35 mm ball nose clears bulk material fast, a 3.175 mm flat end cuts the detail the big tool cannot reach.

Step two

Machined it in three setups

The part has to be flipped between orientations, and every flip is a chance to lose alignment. Dowel-located foam fixtures meant it registered the same way each time. Machining, sanding, painting and assembly were all done in house.

Step three

Measured the surface instead of judging it by eye

We had no profilometer. I traced a profile line across 200x digital microscope images and scaled it to microns against a reference, which gave a number to work against rather than an opinion about how smooth it felt.

Step four

Changed one CAM parameter

Dropping stepover from 12% to 10% brought the machined surface to 14.8 microns Ra against a 15 micron target. Finishing went from 800 grit sanding down to a single 1000 to 1200 grit pass, so the machine was doing work the sandpaper used to do.

Step five

Found the center of mass by testing, not by assuming

Four timed runs per ballast position, averaged. A slight forward bias won at 1.113 s against 1.180 s with the ballast at the rear. Rear was stable but slow off the line, so the fast launch was worth the small loss in control.

Step six

The car that raced

Built mass 48.1 g against a 48.5 g limit. Best run 1.001 s, down from 1.193 s across four builds. Render by the team.

A 44 inch airframe, from CFD to the wing

Aerodynamics and Structures Lead  |  Scope UF, founding member  |  January 2026 to present

We built the airframe from scratch and needed to know where it was losing performance before committing to the build. Hand calculations give lift and drag for a wing on its own. They say nothing about how the fuselage, wing and tail interact.

Step one

Solved the baseline airframe

Full airframe in ANSYS Discovery, air at 12 m/s, steady state and incompressible. Flow stayed attached along the fuselage and over the wing, which was the first thing to rule out. Peak local velocity read 13.8 m/s against the 12 m/s freestream.

Step two

Traced the disturbance back to the nose

Streamlines broke off the sharp tip instead of following the surface, and the disturbance carried back along the forward fuselage rather than settling out. A sharp leading point forces the flow around a corner it cannot follow, so it separates, and the wake behind it costs pressure drag with no lift in return.

Step three

Reshaped the tip into a blended fairing

Curvature is now continuous from the stagnation point back along the fuselage to the wing leading edge, so there is no corner for the flow to leave. In this view it holds against the surface the whole way.

Step four

Re-solved and read the drag

The revised airframe solves to 2.19 N at 12 m/s, read as the X component of total force. Pressure and viscous forces were monitored separately, so a change can be attributed to form drag or to skin friction rather than landing in one number.

Step five

Let the pressure result change the structure, not the shape

Surface pressure concentrated toward the aft wing rather than sitting over the main spar, which is not where the build assumed the load would land. We reinforced the aft section instead of stiffening the whole rib set, putting material where the data showed the load and keeping the weight penalty local.

Step six

Cut the ribs from foam sheet

The Clark Y profile traced onto foam and cut by hand with a knife, one rib at a time. The cut is deliberately left proud of the line. A knife cut in foam tears and undercuts, so the outline is not where the profile is finished, only where it starts.

Step seven

Took each rib to near the line on the disc sander

Fast removal, but it takes material off faster than you can judge it, so the sander stops short of the line on purpose. The rib rides flat on the table so the edge stays square to the face, which matters when ribs are stacked and have to match each other.

Step eight

Finished to the line by hand

The last of the material comes off with 400 grit by hand, down to the drawn outline. This is the step that decides whether the section is actually a Clark Y. A rib sanded under the line at the leading edge or the upper surface is a different airfoil from the one that was solved, and the CFD result stops applying to the part that was built.

Step nine

The rib set and the covered wing

The finished ribs laid out to check them against each other, and the wing section with its skin on. The tape does what film covering does on a balsa build: it closes the surface so the air sees the profile, not the foam. Sanding and covering the wing was hand work, and it is the part of the airframe I can vouch for dimensionally because I made it.
